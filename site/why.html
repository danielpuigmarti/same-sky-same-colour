FREE DATA SHOULD NOT FOOL ANYONE.

NASA’s SPHEREx photos of the whole sky are free for anyone to download. Compared the obvious way, they show changes that never happened. This tool compares them so that a student with a laptop can see how far to trust what they see.

THE PROBLEM

SPHEREx photographs the whole sky in infrared. The colour its camera records changes from one edge of the camera to the other, so the same star is photographed at a different colour each time. Stars are brighter at some colours than at others. Flip between two photos of a star and it can seem to change when nothing happened.

We measured it on 20 steady stars beside RW Aurigae: across all 112 photos of one visit, their brightest reading is 11.8 times their faintest (median).

The trick is old. In 1930 Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto at Lowell Observatory by blinking photographic plates of one patch of sky. A SPHEREx photo of a patch can be at another colour than the last, so a plain blink can show changes that never happened.

WHO IT AFFECTS

SPHEREx’s photos are public. Students, amateur astronomers and citizen scientists can all download them and look for things that moved or changed.

Anyone who compares the photos the obvious way will find “discoveries” that are not real, and can spend a long time chasing them.

WHAT IT CHANGES

It compares only photos taken at the same colour. Between two photos matched in colour the same 14 steady stars swing by a factor of 1.02 (median), a swing of 2 %. A change much bigger than that is worth a closer look, and the “Is it real?” panel checks the usual false alarms.

We tested it on three objects whose motion across the sky is already known, Barnard’s Star and two brown dwarfs. It matched the catalogue’s motion since 2010 to within 0.7 arcseconds each time. Within SPHEREx’s own year the motion is smaller and less sure: WISE 0855 moved 3.2 arcseconds against 4.6 in the catalogue. One pixel of the camera is 6.15 arcseconds. It runs in a browser, with nothing to install, and reads NASA’s public archive directly.

WHO WE ARE

We are two students of applied physics at TU/e, the Eindhoven University of Technology. Neither of us is a professional astronomer, and that is the point.

NASA makes SPHEREx data free for anyone, but free data only helps if beginners can use it without fooling themselves. We want a student with a laptop to be able to look at the same sky as a professional and know how far to trust it. This tool is our first step toward that.
