EVERY CLAIM HAS A CHECK.

Nothing here is for you to do. Before trusting the tool we tested it, and this page is the record: each row is one test, with the rule we wrote before running it (for those that can pass or fail) and what came out, failures included.

In short: of 26 checks, 0 have passed, 4 failed, 4 mark limits, 1 corrected an earlier figure and 17 were run by the code but not reviewed by a person. A person reviewed 0 of 26 within the hackathon, and a check counts as passed only once a person has reviewed it. Where a check failed, its row says what was switched off because of it.

There are 26 checks, for what the site claims: the colour axis, the comet, the five targets, the difference view, the “Is it real?” panel, how a typed position is read, the index of photos, the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, the spectra and the speed of the live path.   6. K4A limitAgrees with a published ratio only within a wide margin, on the same photos (not independent)2.18 ± 0.09 times

    Interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS gave off more carbon dioxide in December 2025 than in August 2025. From the same SPHEREx photos, the December over August ratio of the band at 4.26 µm, corrected for distance, comes out as the published ratio within the combined error.

    Status
    A LIMIT
    Entry
    K4
    Reviewed
    Not yet
    Measured
    2.18 ± 0.09 times
    Reference
    1.88 ± 0.34
    Tolerance
    combined error 0.35
    Pass rule
    PASS if the ratio lies within the combined error of the published ratio (1.88 ± 0.34): |2.18 - 1.88| <= 0.35. The test is weak: the photos are the same ones, a result 20% away would also pass, and the centring of the aperture moves the result by more than its stated error. For that last reason the row is read as a limit, not as a pass.
    What the status means
    This is something the tool cannot measure or claim, said plainly.
    In plain words
    reproduces a published ratio on the same photos (not independent)
    How it was tested
    Flux of the comet in a 2-pixel aperture (12.3 arcsec) with a background ring of 40 to 55 pixels, in the photos of detector 5 (27 in August, 17 in December), the aperture centred on the position in IRSA’s table of the comet’s observations; 6 and 3 of the photos lie inside 4.20 to 4.32 µm. For each pass, a Gaussian is fitted to the flux above the continuum between 4.15 and 4.40 µm, and its area is the band. A band with hard edges is not used: moving its edges by 0.01 µm swings the ratio from 1.25 to 3.04. Scaling: the flux F in a fixed angle on the sky is proportional to Q / (r_h² x Δ), where Q is the number of molecules the nucleus gives off each second, r_h the distance from the Sun and Δ the distance from the telescope (gas leaving at a constant speed); so each photo is multiplied by r_h² x Δ before the fit. The published ratio is 3.0e27 over 1.6e27 molecules a second (Lisse et al.): a ratio of two production rates that those papers derive from their own model of the coma. The pages on disk do not say how, so this test cannot say whether the two quantities are the same; they are the same kind of quantity (carbon dioxide in a 2-pixel aperture on the same photos) and both are scaled to the same distances. With the scaling Δ² in place of Δ the ratio would be 1.47, outside the combined error of the published ratio. The ratio also depends on where the aperture sits: centred on the position from JPL Horizons as seen from SPHEREx (0.3 to 3 arcsec from IRSA’s) it is 2.44 ± 0.09, which would not pass; centred on the light of each bright photo it is 2.41 ± 0.10; with a 5-pixel aperture, which hardly depends on the centring, it is 2.45 ± 0.13, which would not pass. In spring 2026 the band is not there: 3 photos in the band give +0.2 ± 1.0 mJy (3-sigma limit 3.2 mJy) against 18 mJy expected if production had stayed what it was in December; that is excluded at 18 sigma.

WHAT THE TOOL CAN AND CANNOT SEE

An object that moves between two visits leaves a dark patch where it was and a bright patch where it is. The tables give the faintest brightness, in millijanskys, at which that pair shows, for objects of different speeds. “Not seen” means that no brightness that was tried shows at that speed. A made-up moving object counts as seen when its dark and bright pair reaches 5× the noise in at least 80 of every hundred tries. The shift is how far an object of that speed goes in 0.49 years, the typical time between two visits. K10

THE CHALLENGE, LINE BY LINE

What the challenge asks for and what it suggests, in our words, and what this site does about each line. A line is built when everything it asks for works, and partly built when some of it does. As of 15 November 2026.

What it asks for

  • A public web tool that shows images of the sky from SPHEREx.

    Built

    Static files, no server; the deploy workflow publishes them.

    See it: A public web tool that shows images of the sky from SPHEREx.

  • A visitor enters the coordinates of a patch of sky, right ascension and declination.

    Built

    The box reads degrees, hours-minutes-seconds and names, with suggestions while one types, including the string the challenge gives for WISE 0855. It opens a view of that place read live from the archive, with an address one can share; the five targets open at once.

    See it: A visitor enters the coordinates of a patch of sky, right ascension and declination.

  • The tool shows a series of images of that patch like a little video, so that moving objects are easy to spot.

    Partly built

    Blink, curtain and side by side work at any typed position and on the prepared targets, and use only photos taken at the same colour. The film is one visit against another, not a long series; the difference of the two is shown on the prepared targets only, because its test at typed positions failed.

    See it: The tool shows a series of images of that patch like a little video, so that moving objects are easy to spot.

  • The tool is tried on Barnard’s Star, Epsilon Indi B, WISE 0855, RW Aurigae and R Draconis.

    Built

    One item each, with its reference, its tolerance and a plain verdict. The three that move are measured against an image of the WISE telescope taken years earlier; RW Aurigae’s change is given band by band; R Draconis is too bright to measure, and Barnard’s Star too bright to give a brightness.

    See it: The tool is tried on Barnard’s Star, Epsilon Indi B, WISE 0855, RW Aurigae and R Draconis. Epsilon Indi B Barnard’s Star WISE 0855-0714 RW Aurigae R Draconis

  • Anyone can quickly see how the sky changes over time.

    Partly built

    The first page puts one comet and one box in front of a visitor, and the plain-words explanations sit beside each result. Whether people who are not physicists can do it unaided has not been tried yet: the tasks and the pass bar are written, and the session has not been held.

    See it: Anyone can quickly see how the sky changes over time.

What it suggests

  • The tool is easy to update when new data arrive.

    Partly built

    One command, python -m sx.run index, rebuilds the index of photos from the archive’s listing and each photo’s header, and adds the weeks that are new. Tried on one installed week: the same photos in the same tiles, with the last digit of some rows apart. Adding a release that has not been published yet has not been tried.

  • The infrared colours are shown in a way that helps visitors use them.

    Built

    One colour ruler is the key behind every chip, dot and veil; each pixel’s colour is read from the photo’s own tables.

    See it: The infrared colours are shown in a way that helps visitors use them.

  • The data of each of the six detectors are added together to make faint things easier to see.

    Partly built

    Photos of one visit and one detector are added with equal weight. The six detectors are not shown side by side.

    See it: The data of each of the six detectors are added together to make faint things easier to see.

  • Spectra are drawn on the fly.

    Built

    A click on any place, prepared or typed, draws its spectrum from the photos that are loaded, one curve for each visit; every spectrum says that its scale is relative.

    See it: Spectra are drawn on the fly.

  • Visitors can add or subtract images taken at different times.

    Partly built

    The photos of one visit are added together at every position. The difference of two visits is offered on the prepared targets; at typed positions its test failed, so it is switched off there and the page says why.

    See it: Visitors can add or subtract images taken at different times.

  • Visitors choose the field of view.

    Not built

    Every picture has one fixed small size, which is what keeps the download short.

  • Data from other telescopes, such as Gaia and WISE, are drawn over the images.

    Partly built

    Gaia rings mark where fast stars should be, and the cards put an older WISE picture beside SPHEREx. There is no live layer of another telescope.

    See it: Data from other telescopes, such as Gaia and WISE, are drawn over the images.

  • Coordinates in the address let others link to a view or embed it.

    Built

    Position, detector, view and the pair of visits are in the address; the embed switch shows the stage alone.

    See it: Coordinates in the address let others link to a view or embed it.

  • Metadata such as contamination flags is shown.

    Built

    Saturated and damaged pixels are hatched, the pixels of a detected source can be shown, and pointing at a pixel reads every flag it carries.

    See it: Metadata such as contamination flags is shown.

  • Public tools such as Astropy and Aladin are used or offered.

    Built

    Astropy prepares the data and the reference values offline. In the viewer every place, and every point clicked, has links to Aladin Lite, SIMBAD, VizieR, WiseView and the Legacy Survey viewer.

    See it: Public tools such as Astropy and Aladin are used or offered.

  • The data and resources that the challenge lists are used.

    Partly built

    The archive at IRSA is read live, the third data release of Gaia gives the motions of the stars, and every place links to WiseView. The tutorial notebooks and the resources of the Canadian space agency are not used.

    See it: The data and resources that the challenge lists are used.

  • The tool helps volunteers who look for moving objects, as Backyard Worlds does.

    Partly built

    A moving dot gets four checks that help tell a mover from a flaw, and links to ask at Backyard Worlds Talk or the Minor Planet Center. There is no way to send a find in from the page, and no list of candidates.

    See it: The tool helps volunteers who look for moving objects, as Backyard Worlds does.
